01 / The central finding

Actual salaries fall short in every city.

Median take-home pay covers only 74.6% to 86.9% of the amount needed for essential urban living. A salary can be common in the market and still be inadequate.

03 / Why this matters

The gap is paid for somewhere.

When wages do not cover essential costs, workers bridge the difference through family support, borrowing, delayed healthcare, reduced food quality or additional work. Employers may feel the same pressure through disengagement, early-career attrition and difficulty retaining trained talent.

04 / Research approach

A practical benchmark, grounded in local costs.

The study updates the 2024 city baskets with 2025 official price, fare and tariff evidence.

  1. 01

    Define the worker

    One graduate entry-level worker, living independently and without dependants.

  2. 02

    Update essential costs

    Food, rent, transport, utilities, health, connectivity and other basic needs.

  3. 03

    Compare three measures

    Jobseeker expectations, the net living-wage benchmark and verified actual salary.

  4. 04

    Test adequacy

    Measure how much of the city benchmark actual take-home pay can cover.

Evidence base

987 jobseeker expectations + 2,270 verified payroll or worker-earnings records.

05 / From evidence to action

What employers can do now.

01

Use adequacy as a reference

Compare entry-level take-home pay with the relevant city benchmark—not only the legal minimum or competitor bands.

02

Close gaps in phases

Quantify the shortfall, prioritise the lowest coverage and adopt a clear, time-bound adjustment plan.

03

Design benefits that count

Target transport, meals, healthcare, data and housing costs—and measure the expense genuinely replaced.
